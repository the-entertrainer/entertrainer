# The SEWA Chronicles: Narrative Style Options (Pre-approval Record)

## Decision recorded after approval

The user approved a **normal human-written project post**, not a named writing device, an inspirational monologue, or a magazine imitation. The final SEWA reader therefore uses a direct first-person account: it names the Club Mahindra commission, Naveen’s early-career housekeeper role, the real service incidents, the process of making sixteen pages, the original artwork, and the credit. None of the options below are used as a visible framing device in the final book.

## Options reviewed before the rewrite

The current book should not be rewritten as a generic success story. The project has a stronger fact at its centre: while working as a housekeeper early in a career, Naveen accepted responsibility for a sixteen-page comic book about service moments at Club Mahindra. The writing should let that fact gather weight through specific scenes, materials, choices, and pages.

The options below are **original approaches**, not imitations of the reference writing. They apply the useful parts of the research: a clear background, process, and outcome; short captions that orient a skimming reader; and an ending that returns to the opening situation while pointing to the next piece of work.[1] [2] [3]

| Option | Core form | Best use | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. The Floor Notebook | Scene-led first-person account | A reader who should feel the work happen in a real place | Can become too quiet if every page has the same pace |
| B. The Working Proof | One question, one choice, one artefact per spread | A reader who wants to understand craft and responsibility quickly | Can become clinical if the personal stake is not retained |
| C. The Letter from the Making | Controlled retrospective address to an earlier self | A more intimate, magazine-feature reading experience | Can become sentimental or self-conscious |
| D. The Comic Editor’s Notes | Short annotations placed around the original comic pages | A visual-first book where the art does most of the work | Cannot carry the opening and ending on its own |

## A. The Floor Notebook

This approach opens on something tangible from the workday, not a thesis. Each spread begins with a precise observed detail: a notebook line, a staff conversation, a colour proof, a panel that had to be redrawn. The project develops through these moments in chronological order, but each page must introduce a new pressure or decision.

> **Opening sample**  
> I wrote the first note before I knew it would become a comic book. It was a service story from the resort floor, told quickly between tasks. There was a guest, a problem, and one colleague who did not walk away from it. By the end of the day, I had the outline of a page. A few weeks later, Club Mahindra asked me to make sixteen.

> **Closing sample**  
> The last page went to print, but the notebook stayed with me. It taught me that a small observation can carry more weight when you give it a shape people can return to. The next time a piece of work looked larger than my experience, I had one answer ready: begin with the page in front of you.

This is the **recommended primary style**. It is human without performing intimacy, and it gives the book a natural forward pull.

## B. The Working Proof

This is a spare editorial account. Each spread answers one concrete question: *What was the brief? What made a service moment worth drawing? What had to fit on one page? What was changed before print?* The page follows with a decision and an artefact. First person appears only when it clarifies responsibility.

> **Opening sample**  
> The assignment was not to explain service values. It was to make them visible. I had sixteen pages, real stories from the resort floor, and no room for a scene that did not earn its place.

> **Closing sample**  
> The finished comic gave the stories a form that could be read, passed on, and used. For me, the proof was simpler: I could take responsibility for a complete piece of work, from the first conversation to the final layout.

This is the strongest choice if the book should be read chiefly as a **creative case study**. Its risk is a loss of warmth unless it borrows a few grounded scenes from Option A.

## C. The Letter from the Making

This form is written as a restrained retrospective letter to the person who first accepted the commission. It moves between the practical demands of the work and what could not be known at the start. The device should appear only at the beginning, one midpoint, and the end, not on every page.

> **Opening sample**  
> You say yes before you know how long sixteen pages really are. At that point you are working as a housekeeper, carrying a notebook, and trying to learn how a real service moment becomes a scene. Keep the note. Ask the next question. Draw the first panel anyway.

> **Closing sample**  
> You will not leave this work with a grand answer. You will leave with a finished book, a stack of corrections, and a clearer sense of what your hands can hold. That is enough to take the next assignment seriously.

This option is the most intimate, but it requires restraint. It should be chosen only if the reader should encounter the book as a **personal editorial feature**, not primarily as a project record.

## D. The Comic Editor’s Notes

This approach places compact, specific marginal notes around each original comic. The notes do not explain the moral of a story. They describe why the moment became a page, what detail needed accuracy, or what changed between source story and final panel. It works best as a secondary system for the comic-page portion of the book.

> **Sample note beside “Lost Yet Found”**  
> The bracelet story stayed because its turning point was small: someone noticed it, recorded it, and made sure it went back. The page needed that chain of care, not a speech about ownership.

> **Sample note beside “Towel Origami Magic”**  
> This scene needed the child before the rabbit. The drawing works because the page first gives the problem its full space.

This is the best companion to Option A or B. It lets the actual SEWA artwork carry the emotional work while the narration gives the reader just enough context.

## Original recommendation, superseded

The earlier research recommended a hybrid of **The Floor Notebook** and **The Comic Editor’s Notes**. That recommendation was superseded by the approved direct-project-post approach above.

| Book segment | Proposed form |
|---|---|
| Cover | Original cover only |
| Opening and brief | Floor Notebook |
| Finding and shaping stories | Floor Notebook with a Working Proof page for the process |
| Five comic pages | Comic Editor’s Notes, one concise note per page |
| Closing | Floor Notebook, returning to the first notebook detail and opening toward the next assignment |

The reusable copy rules remain applicable: begin with a thing that happened; name only details that move the work forward; keep each spread to one turn in the story; never claim the project was “meaningful,” “transformative,” or “a journey” when a factual scene can show why it mattered.
